Biography

Aziiz Guuleed

Environmental scientist, researcher and senior lecturer. Founder of the Guuleed Green platform for ideas, evidence and solutions for a resilient Somalia.

The work

I am Aziiz Guuleed, an environmental scientist, researcher and senior lecturer whose work focuses on the intersection of environmental science, climate change, governance, sustainable development and public policy, with particular attention to Somalia and the wider Horn of Africa.

I am interested in environmental research, climate science, water quality, pollution, ecosystem degradation, GIS and remote sensing, and the use of environmental data to understand changing landscapes, communities and development patterns.

Guuleed Green is the public home of that work: a place to think in public, to teach beyond the lecture hall, and to argue for environmental decisions that can actually be implemented in Somali institutions, cities and landscapes.

Approach

A Somali environmental thinker, not only a commentator on climate change.

The aim is to become a policy voice: someone who can connect field evidence, classroom teaching and the hard questions of public decision-making.

Science

Evidence before slogans

Climate and environment in Somalia are often discussed as emergency headlines. The work here starts from measurements, maps, water quality, land cover and the lived pattern of drought, flood, waste and urban growth.

Place

Somalia first, regionally connected

The Horn of Africa shares weather systems, river basins, rangelands and displacement routes. Analysis that ignores those connections will misread both the problem and the policy.

Governance

Institutions are the climate story

Who decides how land is used, how water is allocated, how waste is handled, and how climate finance is spent is as important as rainfall data. Governance is not a side topic. It is the work.

Teaching

Research that can be taught

As a senior lecturer, I care about making environmental science usable: for students, civil servants, journalists and community organisations who need more than a PDF they will never open.

Roles

Scientist. Researcher. Senior lecturer.

These are not decorative titles. They describe three ways of working with the same material.

  • Scientist — asking measurable questions about water, pollution, ecosystems, climate and land.
  • Researcher — turning those questions into analysis that can inform policy and public debate.
  • Senior lecturer — training the next generation of environmental professionals in Somalia and the region.

Guuleed Green sits beside those roles as a public platform: essays, briefings, data-driven projects and a channel for independent support.

Areas of interest

Where the work concentrates.

Eight categories drawn from the research, teaching and public work of Guuleed Green. Each one is a door into the same question: how Somalia can manage a changing environment with evidence and fair institutions.

01

Environmental Governance

Environmental outcomes in Somalia are decided as much in offices, courts and local authorities as they are in the field. This line of work looks at the policies, mandates and accountability systems that govern land, water, biodiversity, waste and climate risk — from district practice to national frameworks — and at what it takes to make those rules real.

02

Climate Change & Resilience

The Horn of Africa is already living climate change: failed rains, flash floods, heat, and the movement of people and livestock under stress. The interest here is not climate as a slogan. It is resilience as public policy — early warning, adaptation, livelihoods, and the institutions that must hold when the weather does not.

03

Pollution & Waste

Solid waste, plastics, informal dumping and hazardous materials are reshaping urban and coastal environments. This work treats pollution as an environmental health and governance problem: who generates waste, who is exposed, and which systems can reduce harm without waiting for a perfect municipality.

04

Water & Wastewater

Water quality, scarcity, groundwater, rivers and wastewater sit at the centre of both daily life and national development. The scientific interest is in how water is measured, protected and allocated; the policy interest is in making that science usable for utilities, local government and communities.

05

Urban Environmental Management

Mogadishu and other Somali towns are growing faster than their environmental services. Urban environmental management looks at cities as systems — drainage, waste, air, green space, flooding and liveability — and at how planning can keep urban growth from becoming a permanent ecological debt.

06

Environmental Data & GIS

GIS, remote sensing and environmental data make changing landscapes visible. This work is about using maps and measurements to understand land cover, drought, settlement, water and development patterns — and about closing the data gap that still separates Somali decision-makers from the evidence they need.

07

Climate Communication

Evidence that cannot be explained will not change a budget, a by-law or a classroom. Climate communication here means translating research into public conversation: lectures, briefings, essays and teaching materials that reach citizens and policymakers without flattening the science.

08

Sustainable Development

Development that ignores ecosystems will fail; conservation that ignores poverty will not last. This category holds the practical question of Guuleed Green: which pathways can restore land and water, reduce poverty, and remain honest about the environmental limits of Somalia’s recovery.

Environmental governance

Environmental governance after conflict

Somalia does not lack environmental problems. It lacks the institutions that can hold land, water and climate risk in common.

Aziiz Guuleed · Guuleed Green

When people speak of Somalia’s environment they usually begin with drought. That is understandable. Failed rains still empty wells, kill livestock and move families. But drought is not the whole story, and it is a poor place to stop thinking.

The deeper problem is governance: who is allowed to decide how land is used, how water is shared, how waste is handled, and how climate money is spent. In a country rebuilding public authority, those questions are not administrative details. They are the environmental crisis.

Rules that do not reach the ground

Somalia has strategies, laws and ministry mandates on paper. What it often lacks is the last mile — a district office that can enforce a dumping ban, a water agency that can publish quality data, a land process that can prevent a rangeland from being fenced into private scarcity. Environmental governance fails quietly. Trees disappear. Drainage fills with plastic. A borehole is drilled without a record. Nobody is clearly responsible, so nobody is clearly at fault.

Conflict made this worse, but peace will not automatically repair it. Institutions have to be designed for the landscapes they govern: pastoral corridors, urban floodplains, coastal dumps, river basins shared with neighbours. A ministry organogram copied from elsewhere will not do that work.

What a serious agenda would look like

First, clarity of mandate. Climate, water, waste, wildlife and urban planning cannot all be everyone’s side project. Second, data that is public enough to be argued with. Third, local government that is treated as an environmental actor, not only as a security or revenue actor. Fourth, honesty about customary authority. In much of Somalia, xeer and clan practice already govern pasture and water. Ignoring that fact produces laws that look modern and change nothing.

Guuleed Green exists to keep these questions in public: not as a substitute for the state, but as a place where evidence, teaching and policy argument can meet. Environmental science without governance is a report. Governance without science is a speech. Somalia needs both, held in the same conversation.

Climate change & resilience

Resilience is a development question

Drought and flood are not only meteorological events. They are tests of planning, finance and political attention.

Aziiz Guuleed · Guuleed Green

Somalia is often described as “vulnerable to climate change,” as if vulnerability were a natural trait, like a coastline. It is not. Vulnerability is produced: by where people are allowed to settle, by whether early warning reaches a pastoralist before the water is gone, by whether a city has drainage, by whether a household has a second livelihood when the rains fail.

That is why climate resilience belongs inside development policy, not beside it as a donor theme. A school without shade, a market without drainage, a rangeland without rest — these are development choices with climate consequences.

From emergency to system

Humanitarian response will remain necessary. The Horn will keep producing seasons that overwhelm families. But a country cannot build a future out of successive emergency appeals. Resilience means the boring work: water points that still function in a bad year, urban plans that keep houses off the flood path, seeds and livestock services that recover after loss, and public information that is trusted.

It also means looking at displacement honestly. When drought moves people into towns, the environmental problem moves with them — into waste, wastewater, overcrowding and informal settlement. Urban environmental management and climate adaptation are the same agenda wearing two hats.

What evidence is for

Climate science in this region is not an abstract debate about degrees of warming. It is a tool for deciding where to invest scarce public attention. Rainfall records, vegetation indices, flood extents and water quality measurements should be ordinary instruments of government, the way a budget is ordinary.

Guuleed Green will keep returning to this point. Resilience is not a feeling and it is not a workshop. It is a set of public systems that still work when the weather does not. If Somalia can build those systems, climate change remains a threat. If it cannot, climate change becomes the master story of everything else — poverty, conflict, migration — and we will have chosen that outcome by neglect.

Environmental data & GIS

What we do not map, we cannot protect

Environmental data is not a luxury for Somalia. It is how changing landscapes become visible to policy.

Aziiz Guuleed · Guuleed Green

A landscape can degrade in full view of the people who live on it and still remain invisible to the state. Without maps, time series and shared datasets, charcoal fronts, shoreline change, flood extents and informal urban growth become anecdotes. Anecdotes do not survive a budget negotiation.

This is why GIS and remote sensing belong at the centre of Somali environmental work, not at the decorative edge. Satellites already photograph the Horn of Africa every week. The question is whether Somali researchers, lecturers and public institutions can turn those images into decisions.

The data gap is a power gap

When environmental information lives only on a consultant’s laptop, the country does not own its own diagnosis. Students cannot learn from it. Journalists cannot check it. Districts cannot plan with it. A serious data agenda would treat environmental datasets as public infrastructure: land cover, water points, waste sites, drought indices, coastal change.

That does not mean every dataset should be open without care. Some locations are sensitive. It does mean that “we do not have data” should stop being an acceptable end to a policy conversation. Often the data exists — in UN dashboards, in university hard drives, in a ministry folder — and has never been assembled into a national picture.

From pixels to public argument

GIS is not only a technical skill. It is a way of arguing with evidence. A map of plastic accumulation along a city drainage line is a claim about urban management. A time series of vegetation in a grazing reserve is a claim about land governance. A flood layer over an informal settlement is a claim about who is allowed to live at risk.

My own interest in environmental data is practical. I want students who can read a satellite product; researchers who can connect water quality samples to a place; and policymakers who can no longer say they were not shown the change. Guuleed Green will use maps and measurements as a public language — because what we do not map, we will not protect, and what we do not measure, we will keep calling fate.
