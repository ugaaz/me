Climate change & resilience

Resilience is a development question

Drought and flood are not only meteorological events. They are tests of planning, finance and political attention.

Aziiz Guuleed · Guuleed Green

Somalia is often described as “vulnerable to climate change,” as if vulnerability were a natural trait, like a coastline. It is not. Vulnerability is produced: by where people are allowed to settle, by whether early warning reaches a pastoralist before the water is gone, by whether a city has drainage, by whether a household has a second livelihood when the rains fail.

That is why climate resilience belongs inside development policy, not beside it as a donor theme. A school without shade, a market without drainage, a rangeland without rest — these are development choices with climate consequences.

From emergency to system

Humanitarian response will remain necessary. The Horn will keep producing seasons that overwhelm families. But a country cannot build a future out of successive emergency appeals. Resilience means the boring work: water points that still function in a bad year, urban plans that keep houses off the flood path, seeds and livestock services that recover after loss, and public information that is trusted.

It also means looking at displacement honestly. When drought moves people into towns, the environmental problem moves with them — into waste, wastewater, overcrowding and informal settlement. Urban environmental management and climate adaptation are the same agenda wearing two hats.

What evidence is for

Climate science in this region is not an abstract debate about degrees of warming. It is a tool for deciding where to invest scarce public attention. Rainfall records, vegetation indices, flood extents and water quality measurements should be ordinary instruments of government, the way a budget is ordinary.

Guuleed Green will keep returning to this point. Resilience is not a feeling and it is not a workshop. It is a set of public systems that still work when the weather does not. If Somalia can build those systems, climate change remains a threat. If it cannot, climate change becomes the master story of everything else — poverty, conflict, migration — and we will have chosen that outcome by neglect.
