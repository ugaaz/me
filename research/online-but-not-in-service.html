Online but not in service

An assessment of Somali public-sector websites against the UN Online Service Index framework. They publish plenty about officials. Almost none let a citizen get something done.

Arafat Abdullahi AliAuthor
October 2026Working paper
16 websites, 25 indicatorsFederal, regional and state

Summary

Somalia ranked 191st of 193 countries in the UN's 2024 E-Government Development Index, yet most of its public institutions now run websites. This study asks what those websites actually do for citizens.

Twenty-one federal, regional and state institutions were sampled in October 2026, and 16 sites could be assessed. Each was coded on 25 yes-or-no indicators grouped under the five dimensions of the UN Online Service Index: institutional framework, content, online services, e-participation and technology.

The result is a web presence that is information-rich and transaction-poor. Sites are technically sound and publish news, but they work as press offices rather than service counters. The gap is not mainly about infrastructure: Somalia already has a national ID system and some of the world's highest mobile-money use. It is about how the sites are designed and who owns them.

Eleven of the 16 sites scored zero on online services. Only four offered any online application or lookup: the Immigration and Citizenship Agency (e-visa and passport applications), the Ministry of Education (certificate verification, exam results and appointments), the Central Bank (registers of licensed institutions) and the Puntland Ministry of Finance (a customs tax calculator).

Information is common by comparison. Thirteen sites describe their mandate, 12 name their leadership, 13 link to social media and 10 had posted news within the last 90 days.

Scores by site

The lowest scores fall on the institutions citizens and businesses deal with most: the capital's administration and the ministry responsible for business registration both served only placeholder pages, earning credit for nothing beyond a secure connection and a mobile layout. The Ministry of Interior appears to have gone backwards, showing dated news in 2025 and an "under construction" page in October 2026.

Federal sites averaged 0.43, the two state sites 0.48 and the one regional site 0.08. These groups are too small to generalise from.

Method

Twenty-one institutions were chosen to cover the presidency, the prime minister's office, the main service ministries, the Immigration and Citizenship Agency, the Central Bank, the courts, the Banadir Regional Administration and the federal member states. Five could not be assessed: two sites show nothing until JavaScript runs, and three refused automated access or could not be found. That left 16 sites: 13 federal, 1 regional and 2 state.

The instrument adapts the five subindices of the UN Online Service Index to individual institutions. Each dimension has five indicators, scored 1 when the feature was present and working. A site's score is the share of the 25 indicators it met. All indicators carry equal weight, which keeps scoring transparent but means results can't be compared directly with official UN values.

Institutional frameworkMandate, leadership, laws and policies, privacy policy, full contact details
ContentRecent news, downloadable reports, Somali content, English content, site search
Online servicesService requirements, application forms, online application, online payment, online lookup or tracking
E-participationFeedback form, social media, newsletter, published statistics, consultation tool
TechnologyHTTPS, mobile layout, indexable without JavaScript, no template leftovers, no dead links

Homepages and first-level pages were retrieved on 13 and 14 October 2026 with an automated tool and coded by a single coder.

Limits: a small sample, with three member states missing; features behind logins, JavaScript or deeper links may have been missed, so scores are a lower bound; one coder, so inter-rater reliability wasn't tested; indicators record whether a feature exists, not how well it works; and the results describe October 2026 only.

What should change

The government's National Transformation Plan targets 80% of services online by 2029, up from 23% today. The foundations already exist: the national ID authority has registered more than a million citizens, and over 70% of adults use mobile money. No site in the study used either.

  1. Measure services, not sitesTrack transactions completed online, not how many institutions have a website.
  2. Start where contact is highestLicences, taxes and business registration in Banadir and at Commerce and Industry.
  3. Build one platform, not many sitesA shared portal with common ID and payment, as Kenya did with eCitizen and Rwanda with Irembo.
  4. Set a minimum website standardSearch, a feedback form and a privacy policy cost little and could be required of every institution.
  5. Consider long-term partnershipsService contracts with local technology firms can keep platforms running after a donor project ends.
